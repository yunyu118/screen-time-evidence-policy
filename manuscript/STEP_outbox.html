Dear John,

Thank you for agreeing to help with this. I am attaching the first of two papers from a project I have been calling Screen Time Evidence and Policy, and I would be glad to have you as a coauthor on both.

The paper asks a narrow question: how much of the current youth screen time and social media policy landscape rests on evidence that the intervention works, as distinct from evidence that the exposure is associated with harm. We built an inventory of 13 enacted or announced policies across 10 jurisdictions and systematically searched PubMed and Europe PMC for evaluations of them.

The finding I think will interest you most concerns suicide. Across the 12 published policy evaluations we identified, none reported suicide or self-harm, and only 5 reported any mental health outcome at all. Suicide is the outcome invoked most often in advocacy for these laws and it is measured in none of the studies of whether they work. The exposure level is no cleaner. An instrumental variable analysis of Twitter's geographic adoption finds no relationship with county suicide rates, and a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study finds no effect of device use on suicide risk together with evidence running the other way. Against those, broadband rollout instruments in Italy and Spain do find increases in hospital-recorded self-harm and, in Spain, suicide mortality among adolescent girls. The designs capable of testing the question disagree.

Two other results carry the argument. Korean game-server logs show the 2011 gaming curfew cut play by 109 minutes among light users and not at all among heavy users, who moved more minutes into unrestricted hours than they lost. And the most cited evaluation of school phone bans, reporting a 0.07 SD gain in exam performance, failed to replicate in a larger Swedish sample that rejects gains above 0.01 SD. More than 30 US states are adopting that policy now.

I plan two papers. The first is the attached argument piece, currently drafted to JAMA Special Communication length, making the case that professional bodies should use their authority to require that proposed solutions be tested rather than presenting them as established remedies. The second is a registered systematic review with component meta-analyses, which I am starting now. The individual-level reduction trials can be pooled; the policy evaluations cannot, because they share no common estimand. Demonstrating that asymmetry formally is the point of the second paper.

Three questions, whenever you have time:

  1. Where does the draft overreach? I would rather hear it from you than from a reviewer.
  2. Is the two-paper split the right strategy, or would you keep this as one piece?
  3. The argument paper could instead go as a 1200-word Viewpoint. That is faster and needs no presubmission inquiry, but it allows only one display item, and I think the policy inventory table is what makes the piece evidence rather than opinion. I would value your judgment on that trade.

Any other comments are welcome, including whether the framing is fair to the Surgeon General's advisory and to the professional organizations. I have tried to agree with the goal and to disagree only with the leap from concern to prescription.

Thank you,
